# The Great Human Experiment
Rob Phillips and Kian Faizi

## Course description

Humans are arguably the world’s most powerful evolutionary force. We reshape and remake the Earth by redistributing its matter, its energy, its biomass — perhaps even its intelligence. Thus, we are all participants in an ongoing planetary-scale experiment that will determine the future of our biosphere. And the understanding of this planetary-scale experiment draws from all corners of science and engineering. What are the results so far? In this course, we will learn to use the increasingly powerful data and methods at our disposal to “run the numbers” on key human-driven planetary transformations. Ultimately, the students and instructors together will collaboratively develop and publish a quantitative snapshot of the state of the planet, in much the same spirit of our earlier work on Bionumbers and the Biomass Distribution of the Earth (Bar-On, Phillips, Milo, PNAS).
To that end, we will work across every discipline of science and use every tool at our disposal. No specialized prerequisites; all that is necessary are strong quantitative and computational skills, and a certain willingness to take intellectual risks. Concretely, the course will consist of 3 hours of weekly class time, a laboratory component focused on every student taking responsibility for reliably getting to the bottom of some aspect of the great human experiment, field trips to key sites such as the Los Angeles wastewater treatment plants, power plants and giant refrigeration facilities, and a final project that advances our knowledge of the great human experiment through adding entries to the anthroponumbers database, through writing public-facing vignettes and through a jointly published text on this important subject.

## Proposed syllabus

Given that this is a 10 week course meeting for 3 hours/week, we propose the following tentative syllabus based on the tenet that one of the most compelling ways to understand the great human experiment is following how we use water, what we eat, the power we use and how we reconfigure atoms from their natural setting to the chemical and material world:

1. An introduction to the Great Human Experiment. Our quantitative toolkit: order-of-magnitude estimation, data scraping, and working with dimensionless representations that measure the relative weight of human impacts and natural impacts.

2. Land and water use. Land cover categories, remote sensing, and excavation. Drinking, irrigation, and wastewater treatment.

3. Carbon. The atmosphere, lithosphere, hydrosphere, and biosphere. Introduction to the IPCC report.

4. Biomass and productivity. Livestock, crops, fisheries. Justus von Liebeg and the mechanistic conception of fertilizer.

5. Nitrogen. Nitrogenase, Haber-Bosch, and legume cultivation. Eutrophication.

6. Phosphorus. Special considerations for slow cycles. Eutrophication revisited.

7. Minerals. Mining miscellany. Rare earth elements and electronic waste.

8. Energy. Solar input, technological forecasts, and the limits of thermodynamics. Batteries.

9. Information. Genomes and hard drives. Biospheric and anthropogenic computation.

10. Synthesis. Building a shared geological timeline of human transformations.

Along the way, we also hope to punctuate several topics with field trip visits to nearby facilities where these transformations are literally taking place. For example:

- Wastewater treatment (e.g. Hyperion)
- Power generation (e.g. Scattergood Generating Station)
- Concentrated animal agriculture, environmental remediation, and water use are all highlighted at the Salton Sea

Rather than assign conventional problem sets and exams, we aim to assess student learning via a combination of in-class participation and take-home projects. Classes will consist of lectures, live computational demos, and interactive discussions. Early on, we will also introduce the capstone project which represents most of the grade: each student will prepare and present a short research paper and project, known as a “vignette,” summarizing and analyzing the extent of a particular human transformation of their choice — with the goal of compiling all submissions into a single, coherent body of work, such as a co-authored review.

## Student outcomes

This course targets upper division undergraduates and graduate students from all majors/divisions. It therefore presupposes proficiency in the Core, especially quantitative reasoning and a general understanding of biology, chemistry, and physics. However, it does not assume any specialized expertise. We hope the students bring to the course their own unique interests, approaches, strengths, and ideas; they’ll be empowered to share and teach each other during the term, and to explore them in depth via the final project. In particular, students will:

- Develop a broad understanding of global biogeochemistry and the extent of human transformations on the planet, rooted in first-principles reasoning.
- Describe planetary change using a common quantitative framework of stocks, fluxes, rates, temporal and spatial scales, cumulative change, and dimensionless representations.
- Gain familiarity with major publications, databases, repositories, and sensing modalities used in the physical and Earth sciences via opportunistic data scraping.
- Observe firsthand the technologies involved in modern geoengineering through field trips to sites of interest throughout the LA region, and by starting conversations with people working in diverse relevant career paths.
- Produce a final vignette that combines computational analysis, quantitative reasoning, data visualization, and communicative prose on the transformation/topic of their choice, culminating in shared co-authorship on an open-access publication.
